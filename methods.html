How we teach

Methods

Five stops, from paper and pens to confidence without math anxiety. Citations sit under each step; open a category below for the full research map.

  1. 1 Screen-free

    Sessions run on blank paper with colored pens. Writing and drawing concepts by hand builds familiarity that scrolling rarely creates—and keeps attention on the math.

    Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) on handwriting vs. laptop note-taking.

  2. 2 Concrete Examples

    Concrete → Representational → Abstract

    Hands-on models and relatable real-world examples, then drawings and symbols—so notation stands for something they've already built.

    Agrawal & Morin (2016) on the CRA instructional sequence.

  3. 3 Guided discovery

    Students work problems hard enough to stretch thinking, with support that keeps motivation high—discovery with scaffolding, not formulas dropped cold.

    Kapur (2012) on productive failure / guided struggle.

  4. 4 Explain it back

    Concepts are taught so students can restate them in plain language. If they can teach it back, the idea is understood—not just memorized for the moment.

    Feynman Technique (after Feynman; popularized by Scott H. Young).

  5. 5 Confidence

    Growth mindset and math-anxiety support together: praise strategy over speed, treat mistakes as data, and keep sessions calm so working memory stays open.

    Dweck — Mindset research overview (Stanford; Mindset, 2006); Ashcraft & Kirk (2001) on math anxiety and working memory.

Research behind the practice

Three layers of the same work. Open a category to see the pillars side by side.

01 Session design How the room, materials, and pace are set so attention can stay on the math.

Screen-free, analog learning

Sessions run on blank paper with colored pens. Writing and drawing concepts by hand builds familiarity that scrolling rarely creates—and keeps attention on the math.

Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) on handwriting vs. laptop note-taking.

Mindful pacing

We watch cognitive load in real time and revisit key ideas at expanding intervals— spaced practice instead of cramming—so working memory stays open and memory lasts.

Cepeda et al. (2006) on spaced / distributed practice.

Multisensory learning

Visual diagrams, mnemonics, hands-on demos, and real-world links give multiple routes into the same idea—so more students can access and remember it.

CAST — Universal Design for Learning on multiple means of representation.

02 Building understanding How concepts move from experience to language, symbols, and lasting number sense.

Concrete Examples

We move from hands-on models to drawings and graphs, then to symbols—so notation stands for something students have already built and seen.

Agrawal & Morin (2016) on the CRA instructional sequence.

Flexible Number Sense

Real-world examples give numbers a job to do—boosting motivation and helping students choose strategies instead of treating math as arbitrary rules.

NCTM Principles & Standards on number and operations sense.

Learn the Language of Mathematics

Visual–verbal links help students decode notation, talk through reasoning, and feel fluent in how mathematics is written, spoken, and shared in class.

Moschkovich (2002) on academic mathematical discourse.

Explain it back

Concepts are taught so students can restate them in plain language. If they can teach it back, the idea is understood—not just memorized for the moment.

Feynman Technique (after Feynman; popularized by Scott H. Young).

03 Emotional & motivational conditions How we protect belief and calm so hard thinking stays possible.

Guided Discovery

Students work problems hard enough to stretch thinking, with support that keeps motivation high—discovery with scaffolding, not formulas dropped cold.

Kapur (2012) on productive failure / guided struggle.

Growth Mindset

We dismantle “I’m not a math person,” praise strategy and effort, and treat mistakes as data—building confidence students can measure in how they approach hard problems.

Dweck — Mindset research overview (Stanford; Mindset, 2006).

Math Anxiety Support

Anxiety can steal working-memory space when students need it most. Sessions stay low-stress and encouraging so thinking can reopen and learning can stick.

Ashcraft & Kirk (2001) on math anxiety and working memory.
